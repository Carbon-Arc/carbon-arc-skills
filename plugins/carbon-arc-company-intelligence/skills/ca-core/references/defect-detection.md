# Defect detection: how errors in these panels actually get caught

Read before publishing any sharp move (`ca-core` §4, gate 9).

Four data defects were found on the talent pilots, and **none was visible in the series where it
occurred.** Every one was caught by comparison: against a sibling series, a rival panel, or a published
figure. A broken series does not look broken. It looks like a finding, usually a sharp and quotable one,
which is what makes it dangerous.

## The four tests

### 1. Sibling test: what else should have moved?

A title, brand, artist or market that moves sharply should have company. If it moves alone, ask why.

*(On the actor pilot a franchise sequel's streaming fell 98% in two months while the first film in the
same franchise was flat across the same months.)* One entity moving and its closest sibling not moving
is the signature of an attribution break, not of behavior. Conversely, when **everything** moves
together, suspect the panel, unless they share a real cause (see the shared-title trap in
`setup-talent.md`).

### 2. Floor test: is the post-move level physically possible?

*(After that break, the sequel streamed below a forty-year-old film of the same story while sitting on
two major services.)* When a series settles at an implausible level, the move is an artifact regardless
of how clean the curve looks.

### 3. External test: does the level match a published figure?

Panels are samples; check the ratio once per asset per brief (gate 8).

| On the actor pilot | Panel vs published | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| a franchise tentpole | 0.72x | in family, usable for ranking |
| a mid-budget biopic | **4.11x** | wrong, excluded |

A consistent sub-1.0 ratio across entities is a sample and is fine. **One entity wildly out of family is
an error**, and on the pilot it would have made a mid-budget film the actor's biggest.

### 4. Coverage test: is anything simply missing?

*(On the actor pilot a recent release that grossed nearly $200M worldwide returned nothing from the
box-office asset at any resolution, eight months after release.)* An asset missing a major entity is a
**partial sample of the market**, not a scaled-down copy of it. That is a stronger caveat than "read
rank, not level", and it changes what the tab can claim.

An entity resolving is not evidence it has data, and one empty pull is not evidence it has none (gate 7).
That same title was dropped after one empty box-office call; it was the largest streaming title in the
catalog.

## What the pilots' defects had in common

| Defect | Caught by | If missed |
|---|---|---|
| A title's streaming collapses 98% in two months | sibling test, then floor test | the person-level decline reads far steeper than reality; a published headline was wrong |
| A title's box office at 4.11x published | external test | would have ranked as the biggest film |
| A major release absent from box office and cohort data | coverage test | the largest current title omitted entirely; a tab headline was wrong |
| An income cohort inverts in the final month | sibling test across months | two income bands swap, on the one month the tab was built from |

Two of these were **published before being caught**. Both were found because someone asked a question
that forced a comparison, which is the argument for running these tests as a matter of course rather
than on suspicion.

## What this means for the writing

- **A sharp move needs a stated mechanism.** "Down 98%" is not a finding until you can say what did it.
  If you cannot after the unpredicted-finding sweep (`ca-core` §3 1b), say the cause is unknown, list
  what was searched and ruled out, and mark the series.
- **Prefer ranges to point estimates** where a defect is known but its size is not.
- **Mark broken series in the deliverable** with an event marker at the break and a star on any derived
  figure. Do not silently truncate.
- **Never let one instrument stand alone** where a second could check it. Two instruments agreeing is
  what makes a finding safe to lead with.
